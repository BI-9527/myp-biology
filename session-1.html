Four things, and nothing else

An animal solves the problem of staying alive by eating things that are already alive. A green plant cannot move and cannot eat, so it does something stranger: it assembles itself out of a gas, some water, a few dissolved minerals and sunlight. The word for this is photosynthesis — literally, putting together with light.

Every plant on the school grounds needs all four of these. Not three. Take any one away and the whole system stops, which is exactly the fact the next four experiments are built to prove.

1 Water

Pulled up from the soil by the roots. It is a raw material of photosynthesis, it carries dissolved minerals, and its pressure inside cells is what holds a soft plant upright.

2 Sunlight

Captured by the green pigment chlorophyll inside chloroplasts. Light is not an ingredient — it is the energy that forces the ingredients to react.

3 Carbon dioxide

A gas, only about 0.04% of the air, that diffuses in through pores called stomata. Nearly all the dry mass of a tree came in through those holes.

4 Minerals

Ions dissolved in soil water — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, calcium. They are not fuel; they are the parts the sugar gets built into.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Fertiliser is sold as “plant food”, and that name has taught millions of people something false. A plant does not eat minerals for energy. It makes its own food — glucose — and then uses minerals to convert that glucose into proteins, chlorophyll and DNA. Calling fertiliser food is like calling bricks a meal.

Where does a tree actually come from?

Around 1640 the Flemish scientist Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a willow weighing 2.3 kg in a pot holding 90 kg of dried soil, and for five years gave it nothing but water. The willow grew to about 76 kg. The soil had lost roughly 60 grams. He concluded the tree was made of water — half right, and a brilliant piece of evidence for its time. The missing piece was invisible: most of that new mass arrived as carbon dioxide, through pores too small to see.

How to prove a plant needs something

You cannot prove water matters by pointing at a healthy watered plant. You have to build a comparison in which one single thing is different and everything else is identical. That is a fair test, and it is the engine underneath all four experiments in this unit.

Independent variable

The one thing you deliberately change. There is exactly one.

Dependent variable

The thing you measure to see what happened. Give it units.

Controlled variables

Everything you deliberately keep the same, so nothing else can explain your result.

And the control setup is not the same thing

A controlled variable is a condition you hold constant. A control setup is a whole extra plant that gets everything it needs, so you have something honest to compare against. Plant A in the next experiment is a control setup. Students lose marks for mixing these up more often than for any other reason in Criterion B.

Experiment 1 of 4

Does a plant need water?

Two bean plants of the same species, same height, same pot, same soil, same windowsill. One is watered 50 cm³ every day. The other gets nothing for fourteen days.

Analysis

Water keeps cells swollen and firm. That internal pressure — turgor — is the only skeleton a soft-stemmed plant has, so when water runs out the plant wilts and collapses. Water is also the medium that carries dissolved minerals up from the roots, and it is one of the two raw materials of photosynthesis itself.

Be honest about what this experiment does not show

Plant B wilted. That proves water is needed to stay alive and upright. It does not, on its own, prove water is a raw material of the photosynthesis reaction — a plant deprived of water is dying for several reasons at once, and a dying plant stops doing everything. To show water is a raw material you would need to trace the hydrogen atoms, which is exactly what scientists later did using heavy isotopes of water. Writing this limitation into your evaluation is the difference between a level 4 and a level 7 in Criterion C.
