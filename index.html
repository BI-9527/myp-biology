Statement of inquiry

A living system transforms energy and matter only when every condition it depends on is present — and careful evidence is what tells us which conditions truly matter.

Everything you do in these four sessions should connect back to this sentence. You will be asked to quote it in your reflection.

Inquiry questions

Factual questions have one right answer. Conceptual questions ask how ideas connect. Debatable questions have more than one defensible answer — you have to argue.

  • What four environmental factors does a green plant need to stay alive and grow?
  • What are the raw materials and the products of photosynthesis?
  • What colour does iodine solution turn when starch is present?
  • Which mineral ions does a plant take from the soil, and what does each one build?
  • What is the difference between an independent, a dependent and a controlled variable?
  • How can taking something away prove that it was needed?
  • Why is an investigation almost worthless without a control?
  • How is energy transformed as it passes from the Sun into a leaf and then into your body?
  • Why is starch better evidence of photosynthesis than a plant simply looking healthy?
  • When several factors are needed at once, how does one of them become the limiting factor?
  • If a plant can be grown in water with no soil at all, is soil still important?

Session 1  —  What a plant asks for  |  about 55 minutes

Four things, and nothing else

An animal solves the problem of staying alive by eating things that are already alive. A green plant cannot move and cannot eat, so it does something stranger: it assembles itself out of a gas, some water, a few dissolved minerals and sunlight. The word for this is photosynthesis — literally, putting together with light.

Every plant on the school grounds needs all four of these. Not three. Take any one away and the whole system stops, which is exactly the fact the next four experiments are built to prove.

1 Water

Pulled up from the soil by the roots. It is a raw material of photosynthesis, it carries dissolved minerals, and its pressure inside cells is what holds a soft plant upright.

2 Sunlight

Captured by the green pigment chlorophyll inside chloroplasts. Light is not an ingredient — it is the energy that forces the ingredients to react.

3 Carbon dioxide

A gas, only about 0.04% of the air, that diffuses in through pores called stomata. Nearly all the dry mass of a tree came in through those holes.

4 Minerals

Ions dissolved in soil water — nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, calcium. They are not fuel; they are the parts the sugar gets built into.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Fertiliser is sold as “plant food”, and that name has taught millions of people something false. A plant does not eat minerals for energy. It makes its own food — glucose — and then uses minerals to convert that glucose into proteins, chlorophyll and DNA. Calling fertiliser food is like calling bricks a meal.

Where does a tree actually come from?

Around 1640 the Flemish scientist Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a willow weighing 2.3 kg in a pot holding 90 kg of dried soil, and for five years gave it nothing but water. The willow grew to about 76 kg. The soil had lost roughly 60 grams. He concluded the tree was made of water — half right, and a brilliant piece of evidence for its time. The missing piece was invisible: most of that new mass arrived as carbon dioxide, through pores too small to see.

How to prove a plant needs something

You cannot prove water matters by pointing at a healthy watered plant. You have to build a comparison in which one single thing is different and everything else is identical. That is a fair test, and it is the engine underneath all four experiments in this unit.

Independent variable

The one thing you deliberately change. There is exactly one.

Dependent variable

The thing you measure to see what happened. Give it units.

Controlled variables

Everything you deliberately keep the same, so nothing else can explain your result.

And the control setup is not the same thing

A controlled variable is a condition you hold constant. A control setup is a whole extra plant that gets everything it needs, so you have something honest to compare against. Plant A in the next experiment is a control setup. Students lose marks for mixing these up more often than for any other reason in Criterion B.

Does a plant need water?

Analysis

Water keeps cells swollen and firm. That internal pressure — turgor — is the only skeleton a soft-stemmed plant has, so when water runs out the plant wilts and collapses. Water is also the medium that carries dissolved minerals up from the roots, and it is one of the two raw materials of photosynthesis itself.

Be honest about what this experiment does not show

Plant B wilted. That proves water is needed to stay alive and upright. It does not, on its own, prove water is a raw material of the photosynthesis reaction — a plant deprived of water is dying for several reasons at once, and a dying plant stops doing everything. To show water is a raw material you would need to trace the hydrogen atoms, which is exactly what scientists later did using heavy isotopes of water. Writing this limitation into your evaluation is the difference between a level 4 and a level 7 in Criterion C.

Session 2  —  Light writes on leaves  |  about 60 minutes

Starch: the evidence trail

Here is the problem with investigating photosynthesis. It produces glucose, but glucose is soluble — within minutes it dissolves, moves out of the leaf and disappears into the rest of the plant. You cannot catch it.

So the plant does something convenient. Any glucose it does not immediately need is converted into starch, which is insoluble and stays put in the leaf. And starch has a chemical tell: add iodine solution and it turns a dark blue-black. Orange-brown iodine on a leaf means no starch. Blue-black means photosynthesis happened here.

Why not just look at the plant?

A plant kept in the dark for two days still looks green and alive. Appearance is a slow, unreliable indicator. Starch is a chemical result you can produce in one lesson and see with your own eyes.

Why the plant must be destarched first

A leaf already holds starch from yesterday. If you test it without preparation, everything goes blue-black and proves nothing. Two days in a dark cupboard makes the plant use up its stored starch, so any starch you find afterwards must be brand new.

Run the starch test yourself

Six stages, and the order matters. Work down the list — if you try a stage before the leaf is ready for it, the simulation will tell you what would go wrong in a real laboratory.

    Push it further

    A ii apply

    A student forgets the 48 hours of darkness and tests the leaf straight from the windowsill. What do they see, and what have they lost?

    The fifth condition nobody lists

    Water, light, carbon dioxide, minerals — that is the standard list, and it is about the plant's environment. But a leaf also needs something of its own: chlorophyll, the pigment that absorbs the light in the first place. There is a beautiful way to test it, and it uses no equipment beyond the same starch test.

    The variegated leaf

    Some plants — spider plant, variegated geranium, ivy — have leaves that are green in patches and white or cream in others. The white regions have no chlorophyll. The green regions do. Both regions are on the same leaf, in the same light, on the same plant.

    Destarch the plant, leave it in light for six hours, then run the starch test. The green parts go blue-black. The white parts stay orange-brown. The stained leaf ends up as a map of exactly where the chlorophyll was.

    Extension for the fast finishers

    Magnesium sits at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule. So a plant starved of magnesium cannot build chlorophyll, cannot absorb light properly and cannot make starch — even standing in full sun with plenty of water and carbon dioxide. Hold onto that: it is the thread that ties Experiment 2 to Experiment 4.

    Session 3  —  The invisible ingredients  |  about 60 minutes  |  Experiment 3 of 4

    Does a plant need carbon dioxide?

    This one is awkward. You cannot simply take the air away — the plant would suffocate, and you would have changed several things at once. You need to remove one gas and leave the rest of the air alone.

    The trick is a chemical that absorbs carbon dioxide and nothing else. Potassium hydroxide, KOH, sitting in an open dish inside a sealed bell jar, will soak the carbon dioxide out of the trapped air within a few hours. The oxygen, nitrogen and water vapour stay exactly where they are.

    Teacher demonstration only

    Potassium hydroxide is corrosive and burns skin and eyes. In this unit you observe the setup and interpret the result; your teacher assembles it. The greased rim that seals the jar to the glass plate is the part students most often forget — without it, room air leaks in and the experiment quietly fails.

    Analysis

    The leaf from Jar B contains no starch, even though it had water, light, minerals, warmth and healthy green chlorophyll. The single missing item was carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is therefore a raw material of photosynthesis — and it is the source of the carbon in every sugar, every cellulose fibre and every wooden beam a plant will ever build.

    There are only about 4 molecules of carbon dioxide in every 10,000 molecules of air. A large tree pulls tonnes of mass out of that thin supply over its lifetime, one molecule at a time, through pores you need a microscope to see.

    Experiment 4 of 4

    Does a plant need minerals?

    Soil is a terrible experimental material. It already contains minerals, and you cannot remove just one of them. So this experiment abandons soil completely and grows the plants in water culture — roots suspended in a solution whose contents you decide, ion by ion. This is the same technique that hydroponic farms are built on.

    How a water culture is set up

    • Identical seedlings, same age, same mass, roots washed clean.
    • Each in a boiling tube of nutrient solution, sealed at the neck with cotton wool.
    • Every tube wrapped in foil, because light on the solution grows algae that would steal the minerals.
    • Air bubbled through daily — roots respire and need oxygen.
    • One tube has the complete solution. Each of the others has exactly one ion left out.

    Why this design is powerful

    Because every tube differs from the complete solution in exactly one ion, any difference in growth can be blamed on that one ion and nothing else. That is a fair test repeated five times in parallel, and it is the strongest experimental design in this whole unit.

    It also takes three to four weeks, which is why you are meeting it as a simulation.

    Starve the plant of one ion

    Complete all ions present

    Complete nutrient solution: strong stem, deep green leaves at every level, dense white roots. This is the control.

    Session 4  —  Outputs and impact  |  about 60 minutes

    What the plant makes

    Four things went in. Two come out — and one of them is the reason you can breathe.

    Glucose  C6H12O6

    A sugar. This is the plant's actual food — the chemical store where the Sun's energy now sits. It is made in the chloroplasts, and it never stays as glucose for long.

    Select a fate below to follow it.

    Oxygen  O2

    A waste product, as far as the plant is concerned. It diffuses out through the stomata and into the air. Every oxygen atom in the air you are breathing right now was released by a plant, an alga or a cyanobacterium.

    The oxygen comes from the water molecules, not from the carbon dioxide — scientists proved this by labelling water with a heavier isotope of oxygen and watching where it came out.

    Follow the glucose

    The link back to Experiment 4

    Glucose contains only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Proteins also contain nitrogen; chlorophyll also contains magnesium; DNA also contains phosphorus. Photosynthesis alone cannot make any of those. That is precisely why a plant standing in perfect sunlight, with all the water and carbon dioxide it could want, still turns yellow and stunted in a solution missing one ion.

    Build the equation

    In words, the whole unit reduces to one sentence:

    carbon dioxide  +  water   — light energy, trapped by chlorophyll →   glucose  +  oxygen

    In symbols it has to obey a rule the words can hide: atoms are never created or destroyed. Every atom that goes in must come out. Set the numbers in front of each formula until both sides match.

    Write your conclusion and evaluation  Criterion C iii, iv and v

    Why any of this matters outside the lab

    Photosynthesis is not only the first chapter of biology. It is the process that decides how much food a country can grow, how much carbon the atmosphere holds and how much of a hillside is worth protecting.

    Growing more with the same land

    Commercial greenhouses raise the carbon dioxide inside to around 0.1% — roughly three times the outdoor level — because the virtual lab result you just measured holds in the real world: when light is plentiful, carbon dioxide becomes the limiting factor. Yields can rise by a fifth. The gas, the heating and the lighting all cost energy and money, which is where the argument starts.

    Farming without soil

    Water culture, the technique behind Experiment 4, is now hydroponic agriculture. It uses a fraction of the water of field farming and no soil at all, which matters in a country with hot summers and stressed groundwater. It also needs constant electricity and a technician who understands ion concentrations.

    Forests as a carbon store

    A forest is a slow chemical reaction turning atmospheric carbon into wood. Cut it down and burn it and the carbon returns within hours. This is why the same molecule you tracked through a bell jar turns up in international climate negotiations.

    Food or fuel

    Sugar cane and maize can be fermented into ethanol to run cars. The land, water and fertiliser they use could instead have grown food. Both choices are defensible; neither is free.
