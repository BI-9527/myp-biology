What the plant makes

Four things went in. Two come out — and one of them is the reason you can breathe.

Glucose  C6H12O6

A sugar. This is the plant's actual food — the chemical store where the Sun's energy now sits. It is made in the chloroplasts, and it never stays as glucose for long.

Select a fate below to follow it.

Oxygen  O2

A waste product, as far as the plant is concerned. It diffuses out through the stomata and into the air. Every oxygen atom in the air you are breathing right now was released by a plant, an alga or a cyanobacterium.

The oxygen comes from the water molecules, not from the carbon dioxide — scientists proved this by labelling water with a heavier isotope of oxygen and watching where it came out.

Follow the glucose

The link back to Experiment 4

Glucose contains only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Proteins also contain nitrogen; chlorophyll also contains magnesium; DNA also contains phosphorus. Photosynthesis alone cannot make any of those. That is precisely why a plant standing in perfect sunlight, with all the water and carbon dioxide it could want, still turns yellow and stunted in a solution missing one ion.

Build the equation

In words, the whole unit reduces to one sentence:

carbon dioxide  +  water   — light energy, trapped by chlorophyll →   glucose  +  oxygen

In symbols it has to obey a rule the words can hide: atoms are never created or destroyed. Every atom that goes in must come out. Set the numbers in front of each formula until both sides match.

Virtual lab: measuring the rate

Everything so far has been yes-or-no. A real investigation asks how much, and to answer that you need something countable. Pondweed gives you one: cut the stem, put it under water, and every bubble of oxygen that escapes is one visible unit of photosynthesis.

Light intensity does not fall off evenly

Doubling the distance does not halve the light — it quarters it, because light spreads out over a sphere. Light intensity is proportional to 1 ÷ distance². The meter above shows the intensity actually arriving at the pondweed, which is the value a careful scientist would plot rather than the distance itself.

Your results table

Each reading records three repeat counts and their mean. Repeats matter: a single count can be thrown off by one stray bubble, and the mean of three is far closer to the truth.

Why any of this matters outside the lab

Photosynthesis is not only the first chapter of biology. It is the process that decides how much food a country can grow, how much carbon the atmosphere holds and how much of a hillside is worth protecting.

Growing more with the same land

Commercial greenhouses raise the carbon dioxide inside to around 0.1% — roughly three times the outdoor level — because the virtual lab result you just measured holds in the real world: when light is plentiful, carbon dioxide becomes the limiting factor. Yields can rise by a fifth. The gas, the heating and the lighting all cost energy and money, which is where the argument starts.

Farming without soil

Water culture, the technique behind Experiment 4, is now hydroponic agriculture. It uses a fraction of the water of field farming and no soil at all, which matters in a country with hot summers and stressed groundwater. It also needs constant electricity and a technician who understands ion concentrations.

Forests as a carbon store

A forest is a slow chemical reaction turning atmospheric carbon into wood. Cut it down and burn it and the carbon returns within hours. This is why the same molecule you tracked through a bell jar turns up in international climate negotiations.

Food or fuel

Sugar cane and maize can be fermented into ethanol to run cars. The land, water and fertiliser they use could instead have grown food. Both choices are defensible; neither is free.
